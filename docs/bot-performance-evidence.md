# How trading bots actually perform: the evidence

Date: 25 September 2026. Companion to [trading-bot-research.md](trading-bot-research.md) and [implementation-plan.md](implementation-plan.md).

This document lists measured results, not opinions: what academic studies, regulators, trading platforms, competitions and long-running strategy indices report about the performance of automated and systematic trading. It is organised from the population most like a retail bot builder (frequent retail traders and retail algorithmic traders) through the mechanisms that make bot backtests overstate (backtest-to-live decay), the measured results of LLM and AI-driven trading, and the long-run records of mechanical strategies a retail bot could implement, to the one category of bot that reliably makes money (market making) and why it is out of reach.

**How to read a performance claim before believing it**

1. Is it live money, a forward test on paper, or a backtest? Only the first two count, and only the first includes real fills.
2. How long, how many trades, and how many instruments? A few months on five tickers proves nothing; ask for at least a few hundred trades across regimes.
3. Are commissions, spreads and slippage included? Options and intraday strategies live or die on this line.
4. Is the sample survivorship-free? Platforms delete losing bots; marketplaces show winners.
5. Who verified it? Audited or exchange-published indices beat vendor screenshots and social media.
6. How many things were tried before this one was reported? Without a trial count, a good backtest is close to meaningless.

## Results at a glance

| Question | What the evidence says | Strongest sources |
|---|---|---|
| How many frequent retail traders make money? | In any single year, roughly 10 to 35% net of costs. Over three or more years, about 1 to 5%. Fewer than 1% earn a living from it. | Taiwan day-trader studies (450,000 traders a year), Brazil futures day traders (97% of persisters lost), India's regulator (93% of 11 million options traders lost over three years) |
| Do retail bots and systematic followers do better? | No measured advantage. Copy-trading followers show 44 to 49% profitable over short windows and no positive risk-adjusted return after costs; the only visible track records are survivorship-filtered. | Dorfleitner and others (2018), broker loss disclosures, copy-trading platform data |
| How much of a backtest survives live trading? | Typically a quarter to a half of the backtest Sharpe ratio, with a real chance of a sign flip. Backtest Sharpe explained under 3% of live Sharpe across 888 Quantopian algorithms; 215 bank strategies lost 73% of their median Sharpe going live. | Wiecki and others (2016), Suhonen and others (2017), McLean and Pontiff (2016) |
| Do LLM trading agents make money? | Not as traders. In real-money contests four of six (2025) and 26 of 32 (2025 to 2026) LLM accounts lost; contamination-free benchmarks put most agents below buy-and-hold; published agent returns collapse outside the training data or once costs are modelled (one flagship agent went from a claimed Sharpe of 2.7 to a 72% loss on re-test). As analysts they are measurably useful: near-expert financial search, filing interpretation, sentiment features, forecasting at about superforecaster level. | Alpha Arena seasons 1 and 1.5, StockBench, FINSABER, Profit Mirage, the 2026 audit papers |
| Which mechanical strategies have positive net records? | Monthly index option writing (about S&P return minus 1 to 3 points, a third less volatility, Sharpe 0.5 to 0.65) and slow trend following or moving-average timing (5 to 8% a year, half the drawdown of an index fund, decade-long flat spells). Low-turnover factor exposure, best bought as an ETF. | Cboe PUT and BXM indices since 1986, SG Trend Index since 2000, Faber out-of-sample update |
| Which have no credible positive record? | Intraday breakout and day-trading rules (break-even at normal spreads; the best published strategy fell from Sharpe 1.3 to 0.4 out of sample), 0DTE selling (retail lost about 241,000 USD a day), weekly put selling (earned less than monthly), grid bots, retail market making, copy trading by raw return. | Replications of the Zarattini papers, Cboe WPUT study, 0DTE trade-level studies, Uniswap liquidity-provider study |
| What do the professionals achieve? | Trend-following CTAs 4 to 7% a year with Sharpe 0.3 to 0.6; hedge funds in aggregate trail an index fund; the one near-certain winner is high-frequency market making (Virtu: one losing day in 1,485), which is structurally closed to retail. | SG and Barclay indices, Virtu S-1, hedge fund index comparisons |
| What is a realistic target for a well-built retail bot? | 5 to 10% a year, Sharpe 0.4 to 0.7, maximum drawdown 20 to 35%, from a low-turnover strategy with an economic rationale. Backtests promising more than 20% a year with Sharpe above 1.5 have, in every case examined, failed to replicate net of costs or out of sample. | Sections 3 and 5 |

## 1. Retail frequent traders and day traders: the baseline

A retail bot is a retail trader that trades more often and more consistently. The evidence on that population is large, international and consistent.

| Source | Population and period | Result | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Barber and Odean (2000), "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth"](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers%20current%20versions/individual_investor_performance_final.pdf) | 66,465 US discount-brokerage households, 1991 to 1996 | Average household earned 16.4% a year net against a market return of 17.9%. The highest-turnover fifth earned 11.4%. Gross returns were similar across turnover groups, so the gap is trading cost. | Pre-internet commissions; the cost mechanism, not the exact gap, is the durable finding |
| [Barber, Lee, Liu and Odean (2014), "The Cross-Section of Speculator Skill"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1386418113000190) | About 450,000 day traders a year on the Taiwan Stock Exchange, 1992 to 2006 | Fewer than 1% of day traders predictably earn positive returns net of fees. | [Barber, Lee, Liu, Odean and Zhang (2020), "Learning Fast or Slow?"](https://academic.oup.com/raps/article/10/1/61/5545095) | Same Taiwan data, 3.7 billion transactions | The average day trader loses 24 basis points a day net. | [FCA CP16/40 (2016)](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/fca-proposes-stricter-rules-contract-difference-products) | UK CFD retail clients | 82% lost money; average result minus 2,200 GBP per account. | |
| [ASIC, January 2026](https://www.asic.gov.au/about-asic/news-centre/find-a-media-release/2026-releases/26-004mr-asic-secures-nearly-40-million-in-refunds-to-investors-and-drives-change-after-cfd-sector-falls-short/) | Australian retail CFD clients, 2023 to 2024 | 68% of clients (over 133,000 people) lost more than 458 million AUD in aggregate; 32% were profitable after fees. | [US forex dealer quarterly disclosures, Q2 2026](https://forexdetox.substack.com/p/the-life-and-death-of-a-retail-forex) | Active US retail forex accounts | Share of accounts unprofitable in the quarter: OANDA 66%, FOREX.com 67%, Schwab 64%, Interactive Brokers 54%, tastyfx 66%. | Quarterly snapshots, not lifetime; compiled by a blog from CFTC-mandated disclosures |
| [Bryzgalova, Pavlova and Sikorskaya (2023), Journal of Finance](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jofi.13285) | US retail options trades, November 2019 to June 2021 | Aggregate retail options portfolio lost 2.1 billion USD; most of the loss was trading cost, not direction. Round-trip effective spreads about 8% of option value; retail favours cheap weeklies with average quoted spreads of 12.6%. | The most relevant number for an options bot: the spread is the enemy |
| [de Silva, Smith and So (2026), Review of Finance, "Losing is Optional"](https://academic.oup.com/rof/article-abstract/30/2/489/8301159) | US retail option buying around earnings announcements | Retail loses 5 to 9% on average on options bought before announcements, 10 to 14% for high-expected-volatility events; causes are overpaying for volatility, wide spreads and slow reaction. | |
| [Zerodha founder, January 2022](https://x.com/Nithin0dha/status/1478260639105622019) | Zerodha's active futures-and-options traders over three years | Fewer than 1% earned more than a bank fixed deposit (about 7% a year). | Broker statement, not a published study |

**What this section says.** In every market and asset class studied, the majority of frequent retail traders lose after costs, the share that is reliably profitable over several years is in the low single digits, and the dominant cause is cost (spreads, commissions, taxes), not being wrong about direction. Automation does not change the cost line; it only changes how consistently you pay it.

Verification note: this environment's proxy blocked the primary pages (SSRN, journals, regulators), so the figures were cross-checked across search-engine summaries of several independent sources. Figures marked "single summary" appeared in only one.

## 2. Retail algorithmic traders and bot platforms

There is no audited study that isolates retail traders who run bots. Regulators publish algorithmic volume, not algorithmic profit. The closest observable populations are copy-trading and social-trading users (who follow systematic signal providers), prop-firm challenge takers, and the users of bot marketplaces, all of which suffer from survivorship bias in what is displayed.

| Source | Population and period | Result | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|

**What this section says.** Where systematic retail trading can be observed at all, its results look like frequent retail trading in general: roughly a third to a half of accounts profitable in a given short window, a low-single-digit share reliably profitable over years, and displayed track records that overstate the population because losers disappear. The one measured advantage of following a system is the removal of behavioural errors, and even that is offset when followers pick leaders by raw return.

## 3. Backtest to live: how much performance disappears

Every bot starts life as a backtest. This is the best-measured part of the evidence, and it explains most of section 2.

| Source | Population and period | Result | Caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Wiecki, Campbell, Lent and Stauth (2016), "All That Glitters Is Not Gold"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2745220) | 888 US equity algorithms written by Quantopian users, backtested 2010 to 2015 with at least six months of true out-of-sample data | Backtest Sharpe ratio explained almost none of out-of-sample Sharpe (R squared below 0.025); individual in-sample metrics explained 1 to 2% of out-of-sample results; in-sample annual return was **negatively** correlated with out-of-sample return. The more backtests an author ran, the larger the gap. Volatility, drawdown and hedging were the only predictive features. | The single most relevant study for a hobbyist bot builder |
| Quantopian's history ([Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2020-12-16/quant-trading-platform-quantopian-closes-down)) | Crowdsourced quant platform with 300,000 users, 2011 to 2020 | Point72 committed up to 250 million USD to Quantopian-selected algorithms in 2016. The market-neutral fund underperformed, capital was returned in early 2020, and the platform shut in November 2020. | The largest real-world test of crowd-built bots ended in closure |
| [Bailey, Borwein, López de Prado and Zhu (2014)](https://www.ams.org/notices/201405/rnoti-p458.pdf) | Theory and simulation | With five years of data, trying more than about 45 independent configurations almost guarantees an in-sample Sharpe of 1 whose expected out-of-sample Sharpe is 0; with two years of data, 7 configurations suffice. | Mathematics, not an empirical sample |
| [Harvey, Liu and Zhu (2016), Review of Financial Studies](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article/29/1/5/1843824) | 316 published return factors, 1967 to 2012 | Given how many things have been tested, a new factor should clear a t-statistic of 3, not 2; "most claimed research findings in financial economics are likely false". | |
| [McLean and Pontiff (2016), Journal of Finance](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jofi.12365) | 97 published cross-sectional predictors | Returns are 26% lower out of sample (before publication) and 58% lower after publication. | |
| [Falck, Rej and Thesmar (2022), Quantitative Finance](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14697688.2022.2098810) | 72 published equity strategies | Average post-publication Sharpe decay 43%; later publication year and higher signal complexity predict faster decay. | |
| [Suhonen, Lennkh and Perez (2017), Journal of Portfolio Management](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=2757113) | 215 systematic strategies marketed by investment banks across five asset classes | Median Sharpe 1.20 in the backtest, 0.31 live (a 73% haircut); at least a 50% haircut in every asset class; 30% of strategies had a negative live Sharpe; only 8.4% matched or beat their backtest; the most complex strategies decayed the most. | Professional strategies with professional execution, so a lower bound on retail decay |
| [Huang, Song and Xiang (2024), JFQA, "The Smart Beta Mirage"](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3622753) | Smart-beta ETF indexes, before and after listing | About plus 2.8% a year above the market in the backtested period before listing, minus 0.4% a year after listing net of fees. | |
| [Research Affiliates (2015)](https://www.researchaffiliates.com/publications/articles/767-plausible-performance) | Indexes underlying newly launched US ETFs | Outperformance of about 5% a year in the three years before launch, average performance afterwards. | Single summary |
| [arXiv 2604.18821 (April 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.18821) | 1,726 live systematic strategies from 10 institutions, 2009 to 2025, average 13.7 years of pro-forma and 5.6 years live | A distinct structural break at launch: performance that persisted through the pro-forma period deteriorates sharply in the months after going live, statistically far outside sampling noise. | The magnitude was not captured in summaries |
| [arXiv 2608.27734 (August 2026)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.27734) | Strategies discovered by two frontier LLMs on a 453-stock point-in-time universe and a 39-ETF universe, realistic costs, search budgets up to 100 candidates | Every LLM-discovered strategy failed certification once judged against the agent's own number of trials; passive benchmarks passed. A planted leaky strategy with a Sharpe of 35 passed all statistical tests, so statistical deflation alone does not catch data leakage. | Directly relevant to "let the LLM find a strategy" |
| [Arnott, Harvey and Markowitz (2019)](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3275654) | Protocol | Seven requirements for a believable backtest: an economic rationale stated in advance, a record of every trial, clean data, proper cross-validation, attention to structural change, a complexity penalty, and a research culture that rewards honesty over results. | |

**What this section says.** Expect a live Sharpe ratio between a quarter and a half of the backtest, with a real chance of a sign flip, and expect the gap to be worse the more parameters you tried and the more complex the rule. A backtest Sharpe below about 1.0 on fewer than ten years of data, with an unknown number of trials behind it, is indistinguishable from zero. This is the quantitative reason the implementation plan insists on logging trials, walk-forward testing and a long paper-trading phase.

### 4.2 Benchmarks and papers, and what happens when they are re-tested

### 4.4 Failures and fraud around AI trading

- Sentient Technologies' AI fund closed in 2018 after one year of plus 4% and then losses; the AIIQ ETF was liquidated in 2022; Quantopian, discussed in section 3, closed in 2020.
- In February 2026 an autonomous trading agent built by a software engineer sent about 440,000 USD notional of tokens to a phishing request after losing its conversational state, a roughly 40,000 USD realised loss. This is an agent-safety failure, not a bad trade, and it is why the implementation plan keeps the LLM away from order placement.
- Regulators have moved from warnings to enforcement: the SEC charged a scheme that raised over 14 million USD selling fake "AI signals" ([December 2025](https://www.sec.gov/newsroom/press-releases/2025-144-sec-charges-three-purported-crypto-asset-trading-platforms-four-investment-clubs-scheme-targeted)) and another that raised 12.3 million USD for AI trading bots of which about 3% was ever traded (May 2026); BaFin identified more than 700 near-identical fraudulent "invest with AI" websites in June 2025; FINRA's 2026 oversight report added its first standalone section on generative AI ([report](https://www.finra.org/sites/default/files/2025-12/2026-annual-regulatory-oversight-report.pdf)); ESMA issued a warning on using AI for investing in February 2026. US consumers reported a record 15.9 billion USD of fraud losses in 2025, about half of it investment scams ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/04/new-ftc-data-show-people-have-lost-billions-social-media-scams)).

**What this section says.** As a signal generator, an LLM has failed every real-money, live or contamination-free test found: most accounts lose, published returns collapse when re-run outside the training data or with costs, and results swing by 60 points when only the backbone model changes. The mechanisms are overtrading, long bias mistaken for skill, fragility to prompts, statistics on a few dozen observations, and data leakage that no statistical correction fixes. Where AI-driven money does make money (Numerai, Two Sigma, Man AHL, Renaissance) it is numerical machine learning on cross-sectional data with strict cost and risk control, and even then returns are single digits to mid-twenties with real drawdowns. As an analyst, an LLM is measurably useful: near-expert financial search and document parsing, event and filing interpretation (the FinMMEval winner's edge came from parsing 8-K filings), sentiment features for conventional models, and judgmental forecasting at roughly superforecaster level. The winning designs keep the model upstream of a deterministic decision, which is exactly the architecture in the implementation plan.

**What this section says.** The professionals who do this full time with the best data, execution and staff earn mid-single-digit annualised returns from systematic trend strategies with Sharpe ratios around 0.6, and hedge funds as a group trail an index fund. The only bots with near-certain daily profits are market makers, whose edge is speed, scale and being paid the spread that retail pays. A retail directional bot is on the other side of that trade.

## 7. Synthesis: what this means for your bot

**The base rate is against any frequent trader, automated or not.** Across the United States, Taiwan, Brazil, India, the EU and Australia, the majority of frequent retail traders lose after costs, a low-single-digit share is reliably profitable over several years, and the dominant cause is cost rather than direction. Nothing in the evidence shows that automating the same trading changes that outcome; automation removes hesitation and emotion, and it also removes the pause that stops a human from paying the spread two hundred times a day.

**Backtests are the main way bots fool their builders.** The empirical haircut from backtest to live is 50 to 75% of the Sharpe ratio for professionals and worse for amateurs, and the gap grows with the number of parameters tried and the complexity of the rule. Two practical rules follow: log every trial and judge results against the trial count, and treat any backtest Sharpe below about 1.0 on less than ten years of data as noise.

**LLMs are analysts, not signal generators.** Every real-money or contamination-free test of an LLM as the decision maker has ended with most accounts losing, and every headline paper re-tested outside its training window or with costs has lost its edge. The measured strengths of LLMs are reading and synthesising text, interpreting filings and events, and probabilistic judgement at roughly expert level. Keep the model in those roles and keep a deterministic rule set between it and the broker, as the implementation plan does.

**The strategy families with evidence are slow.** Monthly index option writing and trend following or moving-average timing have forty and twenty-six years of public, independently computed records showing positive net returns, both with modest return (5 to 9% a year), Sharpe 0.3 to 0.7, drawdowns of 20 to 35% in crashes, and long stretches of underperforming an index fund. Intraday breakout rules, 0DTE selling, grid bots and retail market making have either no independent evidence of net profits or clear evidence of losses.

**A realistic target.** A well-built retail bot running a low-turnover strategy with an economic rationale can reasonably aim for 5 to 10% a year, Sharpe 0.4 to 0.7 and a maximum drawdown of 20 to 35%, and should expect multi-year periods where an index fund does better. Its real value is discipline and drawdown control, not outperformance.

### 7.1 Implication for the implementation plan

Your stated preference is intraday trading. The evidence in section 5.3 argues against making that the first strategy: the best published intraday results either break even at normal spreads or collapsed out of sample, and the retail intraday population loses in every study. The plan's phase 1 should therefore change in one of two ways; the choice is yours.

- **Option A (recommended): start slow, keep intraday as a research track.** Phase 1 backtests a monthly or weekly SPX put-write or covered-call rule and a diversified moving-average trend rule, both with realistic costs. These have evidence, cheap data needs, and low operational risk, and they exercise the same plumbing (risk manager, approval gate, LLM reporting). Intraday strategies run as paper-only experiments with a pre-declared kill criterion (for example, walk-forward Sharpe below 0.5 after costs for 60 sessions ends the experiment).
- **Option B: intraday first, framed as a bounded experiment.** Keep the intraday plan, but treat the first six months as tuition with a fixed loss budget (for example 2,500 EUR of realised losses ends the experiment), use only liquid large caps and SPX, model half the spread plus slippage on every fill, and require a walk-forward Sharpe above 1.0 after costs before any live capital.

Either way, the numbers to hold yourself to are the ones in this document, not the ones in a vendor's backtest.

### 7.2 What the rare winners have in common

- Low turnover, so costs are a small fraction of gross profit.
- A risk premium or behavioural effect with an economic reason to exist (crash insurance for option writers, slow-moving capital for trend followers), rather than a pattern found by search.
- Few parameters, few trials, and years of out-of-sample data before real money.
- Diversification across instruments and time, not concentration in one ticker or one month of the year.
- Ruthless cost discipline: limit orders, liquid instruments, no leverage until the strategy has paid for itself.
- Honest measurement: live slippage compared with the backtest every month, and a pre-agreed rule for when to stop.
