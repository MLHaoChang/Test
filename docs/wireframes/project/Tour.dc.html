Hindsight bias In plain words: judging a decision by an outcome you could not have known at the time. Testing "add NVIDIA in 2021" today is easy to make look brilliant because you already know what happened; the question is whether the rule that would have picked NVIDIA also picks well when you do not know the ending. Precisely: selection of test inputs conditioned on realised outcomes, a form of look-ahead bias. It inflates historical results and is the main reason backtests overstate live performance (see the evidence page in the docs). In this tool: every scenario that names specific stocks carries a hindsight warning. To remove it, run the rule on a period you have not looked at, or let a scenario agent run it forward from today with fake money and check back in six months. Related: scenario agent, holdout, trial budget, validation (advanced).
Explain this page: Scenarios
What you are looking at Your saved what-ifs and a form to create one. A scenario is your real portfolio plus a change (add, swap, remove, reweight) plus a rule, replayed over past years with real prices and costs. How to read it Look at the end value last. First look at the worst fall, the months to recover, and the "why" text. A scenario that wins by one stock you already knew about tells you little; one that wins by a rule across many stocks tells you more. What to do next Compare two or three scenarios with your portfolio. If one convinces you, run it forward with a scenario agent (fake money) or turn it into a proposal for your approval. Ask the assistant to explain any difference. Common mistakes Adding a stock you already know did well (hindsight warning). Ignoring the concentration warning because the end value looks good. Trusting a five-year result with one market cycle in it.
